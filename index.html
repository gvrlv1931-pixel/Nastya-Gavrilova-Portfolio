About Me

I'm Nastya. I help start-ups and small businesses turn circular economy ideas into everyday practice. The climate crisis is the most urgent challenge we face, and how businesses use materials is one of the most practical places to act.

In my experience, circular projects rarely fail on the material. They fail on people and process: nobody owns it, the old way is easier, or the habit never forms. That's where I work. I map the system, find the friction, and design the roles, routines and incentives that make the circular option the default. Then I help the team through the change.

I bring three lenses. People and culture: an MSc in International HRM from Strathclyde, with a dissertation on culture and inclusion in a Scottish tech start-up. Environmental science: a BSc in Environmental Geoscience from Edinburgh and co-authored published research on biochar. Operations: four years as a shift leader, including leading a team of 87.

Small businesses are where change sticks fastest, because the people deciding work next to the people doing. I've been on that side too. As founder of StuffShare, a sharing and borrowing platform, I know the tight budgets, the many hats, and how little time there is for anything that doesn't earn its place.

Outside of that, I'm usually up a mountain, through a new culture, or deep in a conversation that shifts something. I backpack, hitchhike, host strangers on my couch, and say yes to things I don't yet know how to do. My current one is improv.

CS 01 Partnership opened · not embedded

Retired climbing harnesses: why a good idea needs an owner

At Glasgow Life's Kelvin Hall, harnesses from the Clip 'n Climb centre were retired with no reuse route. Within weeks of starting a part-time frontline role, I found a Highlands business that upcycles climbing gear and opened a partnership. As far as I know, it didn't go further. Why is the useful part.

Organisation
Glasgow Life, Kelvin Hall
Waste stream
Retired climbing harnesses
Partner
Climbing gear upcycler, Scottish Highlands
My role
Activity Assistant, part-time

Proposed route

  1. Harness in use
  2. Retired: end of safe life
  3. Upcycler, Highlands
  4. Upcycled goods

The problem

Harnesses are safety equipment. They're retired on age, use and inspection, often while they still look fine. Once retired, they can't be donated or resold for climbing. With webbing, foam padding and metal buckles stitched together, standard recycling can't take them either. So the only route was the bin.

What I did

  • Spotted the gap on shift and logged it alongside other improvement ideas for the venue.
  • Found a Highlands business that upcycles climbing gear, reached out, and opened a conversation about taking our retired harnesses.
  • Proposed other fixes in parallel, including a Microsoft 365 prototype to automate annual leave and cut paper-based admin.

Outcome

I left in December 2025. As far as I know, the partnership didn't go further. The annual leave prototype stalled for a related reason: rolling it out would have needed sign-off across every Glasgow Life venue, because a large organisation needs consistency.

// critical lens

Why it stalled

  • No owner. I was part-time, on the frontline, and there for under three months. Nobody with budget or sign-off took it on, so it stayed a conversation.
  • Consistency beats speed. In a large organisation, a change at one venue has to work for all of them. That's sensible, but it's slow, and small ideas die waiting.
  • Safety limits the options. A retired harness must never end up back on a climber, so straight reuse is off the table. Only material routes are safe.
  • Small weight, real precedent. Harnesses are light, so the waste saved is modest. The value is a route other retired kit could follow.

What I'd do differently

  • Find a manager to own it before contacting the partner.
  • Bring a one-page case: cost, risk, and who does what.
  • Pilot one tracked batch at one venue, then ask for rollout.

Relevant if you

run a sport, leisure or outdoor venue that retires safety kit, or lead a growing team where good ideas from staff go nowhere.

  • Change management
  • Partnership development
  • Stakeholder management
  • Waste hierarchy
  • Frontline innovation
CS 02 In development · NPD

Spent coffee at a distillery: designing for small volumes

The coffee machines at Jackton Distillery produce a small, steady stream of spent coffee pucks. I proposed a product that uses them, and it's now in new product development (NPD).

Organisation
Jackton Distillery
Waste stream
Spent coffee pucks
Scale
Small, daily volumes
My role
Brand Representative, part-time

Where the pucks go

  1. Coffee machine
  2. Spent pucks: small, daily
  3. New product: in NPD

The problem

Spent coffee is a well-known waste-to-resource story, and there are companies that collect it at scale. Those schemes are built for volume. A few machines don't produce enough to be worth a collection, so the pucks go out with everything else. Any solution has to start from small volume.

What I did

  • Spotted the stream while working as a Brand Representative.
  • Proposed a product built around small volumes, rather than chasing a collection scheme built for industrial quantities.
  • The idea was taken forward into NPD.

Where it stands

No impact figures yet. Until the product works, any number would be a guess. The product details are the distillery's to share, so I'll add them once they're public.

// critical lens

Questions the NPD has to answer

  • Where do the pucks go now? If they already go to anaerobic digestion (AD) with food waste, the product has to beat AD, not landfill.
  • Can they be stored without spoiling? Spent coffee is wet and goes mouldy within days. Drying it with extra energy can cancel out the benefit.
  • Does anyone want it? Having the waste isn't the same as having a market. Test demand before scaling.
  • Is it the biggest loop? Next to a distillery's main by-products, coffee is tiny. Its value is as a visible, low-risk first step that builds the habit for bigger ones.

What success looks like

  • It beats the current route on carbon, not just on story.
  • Storage needs no extra energy.
  • Staff keep doing it once the novelty wears off.

Relevant if you

run a café, office or production site with a waste stream that's real but too small for a commercial collection.

  • New product development
  • Innovation
  • Waste hierarchy
  • Systems thinking
  • Culture change
