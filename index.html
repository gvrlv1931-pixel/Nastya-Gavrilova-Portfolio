About Me

I'm Nastya, a systems entrepreneur building circular economy infrastructure. I come at it from three directions: HR and people operations, environmental science, and community organising.

The climate crisis is the most urgent challenge we face, and it is the one that sets everything else off balance. That's where I want to contribute. My approach is systems thinking anchored in operations and management: looking at the bigger picture, asking what's missing, finding the leverage points, and building the processes and tools that remove the friction. I want to help build a world where people are a part of the planetary ecosystem, living well alongside other species in our one shared home.

Circular Economy Case Studies

Both projects started with the same kind of problem: a waste stream too small or too awkward for mainstream recycling to bother with. Here's what I did, and the trade-offs I'd want any client to know about.

CS 01 Partnership established

A second life for retired climbing harnesses

Climbing harnesses at Glasgow Life were being retired with nowhere to go but the bin. I built a partnership with a small Highlands business that upcycles used climbing gear, so they get a second life instead.

Organisation
Glasgow Life
Waste stream
Retired climbing harnesses
Partner
Small upcycling business, Scottish Highlands
My role
Built the partnership

Where the harnesses go

  1. Harness in use
  2. Retired: end of safe life
  3. Upcycler, Highlands
  4. Upcycled goods

The problem

Harnesses are safety equipment. They're retired based on age, use and inspection, often while they still look fine. Once retired they can't be donated or resold for climbing: the whole point of retiring them is that nobody should trust them with a fall again. And with nylon webbing, foam padding and metal buckles stitched together, they're not something standard recycling can take. So the default route was general waste.

What I did

  • Noticed retired harnesses were leaving with the general waste, with no better route in place.
  • Found a small business in the Highlands that already upcycles used climbing equipment, so the material was a natural fit for what they make.
  • Set up the partnership, linking a large public-sector organisation with a micro-business, so retired harnesses go to them instead of the bin.

Outcome

Retired harnesses now have a route into new products instead of the waste stream, and Glasgow Life has a model it could apply to other retired kit.

// critical lens

What makes this harder than it looks

  • Safety comes before circularity. Reuse sits at the top of the waste hierarchy, but it's off the table here. A retired harness must never end up back on a climber, so the only safe routes are ones where it stops being a harness.
  • The weight is small. Harnesses are light, so even a big batch doesn't add up to much. Judged on tonnage alone, this is a modest win. The bigger value is that a route now exists, and it can take other retired kit too.
  • Distance eats into the benefit. Glasgow to the Highlands is a long way to send a few harnesses. It only makes sense if kit is collected and sent in batches.
  • Big organisation, small partner. Public-sector paperwork can easily outweigh a micro-business's capacity. The partnership has to stay light-touch to last.

Next steps I'd recommend

  • Log every batch (units and kilos) so the impact can be reported, not estimated.
  • Extend the same route to ropes and other retired kit across venues.
  • Write the route into the decommissioning procedure, so it doesn't depend on one person remembering it.

Relevant if you

run sport, leisure or outdoor facilities that retire safety kit, or have any waste stream that's too awkward for standard recycling and too small for anyone to have dealt with yet.

  • Supplier sourcing
  • Waste hierarchy
  • Public sector

CS 02 In development · NPD

Designing a use for small batches of spent coffee

The coffee machines at Jackton produce a small but steady stream of spent coffee pucks. I proposed a way to turn them into a product, and the idea is now in new product development (NPD).

Organisation
Jackton
Waste stream
Spent coffee pucks
Scale
Small, daily volumes
My role
Proposed the idea

Where the pucks go

  1. Coffee machine
  2. Spent pucks: small, daily
  3. New product: in NPD

The problem

Spent coffee is one of the best-known waste-to-resource stories, and there are companies that collect it at scale. But those schemes are built for volume. A few machines don't produce enough to be worth a collection, so the pucks go out with everything else. Any solution has to start from small volume.

What I did

  • Spotted the stream: small, but steady and predictable, which makes it easy to plan around.
  • Proposed a product built around those small volumes, rather than trying to squeeze them into a system designed for industrial quantities.
  • The idea was taken forward and is now at the NPD stage.

Where it stands

No impact figures yet, and that's deliberate. Until the product works, any number for waste diverted would be a guess, and I'd rather not greenwash my own project. I'll share the product itself once it's further along.

// critical lens

Questions the NPD has to answer

  • What's the real baseline? In Scotland, most food businesses already have to separate food waste, and it usually goes to anaerobic digestion (AD) or composting. So the comparison that matters is product vs AD, not product vs landfill. The new use has to beat that.
  • Can it be stored without spoiling? Spent coffee is wet and can go mouldy within days. Storage is where a project like this lives or dies, and drying it with added energy can cancel out the benefit.
  • Is the input consistent? Different blends, and whatever else ends up in the same bin, change what you're working with. The product needs a spec the waste can actually meet.
  • Does anyone want it? Having the waste isn't the same as having a market. Demand needs testing with real conversations before anything scales.

What success has to look like

  • It beats the AD baseline on carbon, not just on story.
  • Storage works without extra energy going in.
  • People would choose it, or pay for it, on its own merits.

Relevant if you

run a café, canteen or office with a waste stream that's real but too small for a commercial collection to care about.

  • Life-cycle thinking
